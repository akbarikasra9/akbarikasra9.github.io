Texas Change Atlas: Methods

This file holds the full definitions behind PLAN.md so anyone can reproduce our results.

County data (ACS Table S0501)

We download Table S0501 by hand from data.census.gov as CSV files for all Texas counties and the state of Texas, for the 2019 and 2024 ACS 5-year subject tables (the R script can also pull the same table with tidycensus::get_acs() if a Census API key is saved). The year 2019 means the 2015–2019 window, and 2024 means the 2020–2024 window. Column C01 holds totals, column C02 holds native-born residents, and column C03 holds foreign-born residents. Rows are found by their labels using load_variables(), and the script prints the rows it used so the team can check them. If a year's table has no single "bachelor's degree or higher" row, the bachelor's and graduate or professional degree rows are added together, with their standard errors combined as the square root of the sum of squares. Counties come from the 2023 USDA file, and county boundaries for the map come from the Census Bureau's 2023 cartographic boundary files through the tigris package. In the USDA Rural-Urban Continuum Codes, code 1 is large metro, codes 2 and 3 are smaller metro, and codes 4 through 9 are nonmetro (rural).

Arrival data (IPUMS USA, ACS 1-year)

The extract is built by hand on usa.ipums.org and saved as a fixed-width file with its DDI codebook. The samples are us2015a through us2019a and us2021a through us2024a, limited to Texas with the case selection STATEFIP equal to 48. Variables are YEAR, AGE, SEX, BPL (birthplace), CITIZEN, YRIMMIG (year of immigration), EDUC, EMPSTAT, INCWAGE (wage and salary income), WKSWORK2 (weeks worked), UHRSWORK (usual hours worked), CPI99 (inflation adjuster), PERWT (person weight), and REPWTP (80 replicate weights). Full-time, year-round means WKSWORK2 equals 6 (50 to 52 weeks) and UHRSWORK is 35 or more, with positive INCWAGE. A person is U.S.-born if BPL is 1 to 120 (U.S. states and territories, including Puerto Rico) or CITIZEN equals 1 (born abroad to American parents). A person is an immigrant if BPL is 150 to 899 and they arrived between 1980 and 2019 at age 18 or older, so the comparison focuses on adults who finished school before arriving. Arrival cohorts are five-year groups (1980–84, 1985–89, and so on), and years in the U.S. is the survey year minus the cohort's middle year.

Generation data (IPUMS CPS, CPS ASEC)

The extract is built by hand on cps.ipums.org and saved the same way. The samples are cps2015_03s through cps2019_03s and cps2022_03s through cps2024_03s, limited to Texas with STATEFIP equal to 48. The survey asks about the previous year's income, so earnings cover 2014–2018 and 2021–2023. Variables are YEAR, AGE, SEX, NATIVITY, EDUC, EMPSTAT, LABFORCE, INCWAGE, WKSWORK1, UHRSWORKLY, CPI99, ASECWT (person weight), and REPWTP (160 replicate weights). NATIVITY code 5 is first generation, codes 2, 3, and 4 are second generation, and code 1 is third generation or later. A bachelor's degree or higher means EDUC is 111, 123, 124, or 125. Full-time, year-round means WKSWORK1 is 50 or more and UHRSWORKLY is 35 or more. A person is unemployed if EMPSTAT is 20, 21, or 22, counted only among people in the labor force (LABFORCE equals 2).

Comparison groups

In the county view, the comparison group is native-born residents. In the arrival view, it is U.S.-born workers reweighted to match the immigrant group's age mix, because older workers usually earn more and we do not want aging to look like catching up. In the generation view, it is third-generation-or-later workers with the same education and sex, so the gap reflects generation rather than schooling or the gender pay gap. For the degree and unemployment gaps in the generation view, the comparison is third-generation-or-later Texans of the same sex, since education is the outcome being measured. All earnings are multiplied by CPI99 before years are combined, so every year is in constant dollars.

Uncertainty and reliability

For microdata, each estimate is calculated once with the main weight and again with every replicate weight. The standard error is the square root of (4 divided by the number of replicate weights) times the sum of squared differences between each replicate estimate and the main estimate. This is the successive-difference method the Census Bureau designed these weights for. For the county table, the standard error is the published MOE divided by 1.645. An estimate is "use with caution" if it comes from fewer than 100 people or its standard error is more than 30 percent of the estimate. For county data, this test is applied to the foreign-born and native-born values themselves, not the gap, because a gap near zero would fail the test even when measured well. The same logic applies to the generation degree and unemployment gaps, where the test uses the group's own rate. A change between periods is clear when it is larger than 1.645 times the square root of the sum of the two periods' squared standard errors.
